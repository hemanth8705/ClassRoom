THE PROBLEM

Every app wants a password. That's a bad deal.

Say you're building a calendar app. Your users have to sign up, so you store a password for each one of them.

Now you're sitting on a database that hackers would love to steal. Most people use the same password everywhere, so when your database leaks, their other accounts go down with it. You'd also have to build password resets, and tell everyone when you get breached. And making people create yet another email and password is a bad experience on its own.

Continuing with Google is much simpler. Google already verifies your users. Your user just tells Google, "vouch for me," and Google tells your app that this is a real person.

That's OAuth. You hand the login job to a company your users already trust. Your app never sees a password. All it gets is a token that says: Google checked this person, and they're allowing you to see these details.

Think of a hotel keycard. You don't hand a guest the key to your house. The front desk prints a card that opens room 204 only, and stops working at checkout. Google is the front desk. The token is the keycard.

Now let’s build it

BUILD · 1 OF 2

First, register your app with Google

Before any of this works, Google has to know your app exists. In the Google Cloud Console, open APIs & Services, then Credentials, and create an OAuth client ID (type: Web application). Microsoft and other platforms have the same step. You get two things back:

Client ID

Your app's public name. It goes in the login URL, and it's fine if everyone sees it.

Client secret

Your app's password. It stays on your server. It never goes in a URL, in the browser, or in Git.

Then you register a redirect URI, like http://localhost:3000/callback. It's the only address Google will send users back to. If a request doesn't match it exactly, Google refuses, so nobody can redirect your users' codes to their own site.

Scopes: what you're asking for

Scopes are the permissions your app asks for, and your app lists them in the login URL. Basic ones give you the user's name, email and profile photo. Privileged ones, like reading their calendar or Drive files, need an explicit yes from the user. Pick a few and see what your user would see.

Ask for the least you need. People click through a name-and-email screen. They close a "delete your Drive files" screen. Google also reviews apps that ask for sensitive or restricted scopes before the public can use them, so while you're testing, add your own account as a test user.

  WHAT CAN GO WRONG
  
Two small checks stop two real attacks

  
Remember state and code_challenge from the login URL? This is why they're there. Switch a defence on or off, then run the attack and see what happens.


  

    

      
1. The fake callback link

      
An attacker signs in with their own Google account, grabs the callback link with their code in it, and sends it to you. You click it.

      


      
      

    

    

      
2. The stolen code

      
A browser extension, or a leaked log, copies the code from the URL and races your app to Google.

      


      
      

    

  

  
In short: your app makes up a random state, saves it, and checks that Google returns the same one. With PKCE, your app makes up a secret (code_verifier), sends only its hash (code_challenge) at the start, and reveals the secret when it swaps the code. Whoever started the login is the only one who can finish it.







  ONE LAST THING
  
Authentication vs authorization

  
People mix these two up all the time. Here's the difference.

  

    
Authentication
Who are you? It's like showing your passport at the hotel desk.
In our flow, that's the ID token. This part is called OpenID Connect (OIDC), a thin layer on top of OAuth. It's what openid email profile switched on.

    
Authorization
What are you allowed to touch? It's like a keycard that only opens room 204.
In our flow, that's the access token and its scopes. This is OAuth itself.

  

  
"Continue with Google" does both at once. You prove who you are, and you decide what the app may do on your behalf.
