THE PROBLEM

Every app wants a password. That's a bad deal.

Say you build a calendar app. Users sign up, so you store a password for each one. Now you own a database people want to steal. Users reuse that password everywhere. You also need reset emails, lockouts and breach notices.

Google already did all of that, better than you will. So skip it. Ask Google: "is this person real? Vouch for them."

That's OAuth. You hand the login job to a company the user already trusts. The user signs in on Google's page. Your app only gets a token that says "Google checked this person, and they allowed you to see X."

Think hotel keycard. You don't give a guest your house key. The front desk prints a card that opens room 204 only, and dies at checkout. In OAuth, Google is the front desk. The access token is the keycard.

Why the last chunk matters

The third chunk is Google's signature. Edit the email, then check. Your backend would compare the signature against Google's public keys and reject a changed token.

This check is simulated here. In real code, use your Google client library to verify an ID token. Don't write the signature math yourself.

Now let’s build it

BUILD · 1 OF 2

Register your app with Google

Google has to know your app exists. In Google Cloud Console › APIs & Services › Credentials, create an OAuth client ID (type: Web application). You get two things back:

Client ID

Your app's public name. It goes in the login URL. Everyone can see it. That's fine.

Client secret

Your app's password. It stays on your server. It never goes in a URL, in the browser, or in Git.

You also register a redirect URI, like http://localhost:3000/callback. It's the only address Google will send users back to. If it doesn't match exactly, Google refuses. This stops anyone from redirecting your users' codes to their own site.

Scopes: what you're asking for

Your app asks for scopes in the login URL. Each scope is one permission. Pick some and watch the screen your user will see.

  WHAT CAN GO WRONG
  
Two small lines of code that stop two real attacks

  
You saw state and code_challenge in the URL. Toggle the defence, then run the attack.


  

    

      
1. Fake callback link

      
An attacker logs in with their own Google account, grabs the callback link with their code, and sends it to you. You click it.

      


      
      

    

    

      
2. Stolen code

      
A malicious browser extension or a leaked log copies the code from the URL, then races your app to Google.

      


      
      

    

  

  
How they work. state: your app invents a random value, stores it, and checks Google returns the same one. PKCE: your app invents a secret (code_verifier), sends only its hash (code_challenge) at the start, and reveals the secret at the token swap. Whoever started the login is the only one who can finish it.







  ONE LAST THING
  
Authentication vs authorization

  
People mix these up. OAuth is the second one. Mostly.

  

    
Authentication
Who are you? Like showing your passport at the hotel desk.
In our flow: the ID token. This part is called OpenID Connect (OIDC), a thin layer on top of OAuth. It's what openid email profile turned on.

    
Authorization
What may you touch? Like a keycard that only opens room 204.
In our flow: the access token and its scopes. This is OAuth itself.

  

  
"Continue with Google" does both at once. You prove who you are, and you decide what the app can do on your behalf.
